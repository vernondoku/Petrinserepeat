Goldendoodles, Labradoodles, Bernedoodles, Aussiedoodles. Kansas City is full of them, and they've become one of the most common dogs on our grooming schedule. They're also one of the most misunderstood when it comes to coat care. A lot of new doodle owners expect a low-maintenance dog and end up surprised by how much brushing a fluffy coat takes.

This post covers what you actually need to know: how doodle coats vary, how to tell the groomer what you want, which lengths fit which lifestyles, what happens when the coat isn't in great shape, and how to decide between PRR's van and the North Kansas City salon.

## Not all doodles have the same coat

"Doodle" describes a cross, not a coat. Depending on which parent the puppy favors and the generation of the cross, a doodle's coat might be:

- **Curly and dense**, close to a poodle. Sheds very little, tangles fast, needs the most brushing.
- **Wavy**, the classic teddy-bear look. Still tangles readily, especially where the coat rubs.
- **Straight or loose**, closer to the retriever side. Sheds more and often tangles less, but the fine undercoat can still mat.

Two dogs from the same litter can land on different points of that range. That's why a groomer wants to see and feel the coat before promising a specific finish. The style that worked for your neighbor's doodle may not be achievable, or practical, on yours.

## Bringing an inspiration photo (and what to expect from it)

Inspiration photos are genuinely useful. They tell your groomer what you like more clearly than words like "fluffy but not too fluffy." Bring one or show it on your phone at the appointment.

Then expect an honest conversation. A good groomer looks at the photo and your dog and tells you one of three things:

1. **"We can do that."** The coat is in good shape and the style suits your dog's build.
2. **"We can do a version of that."** Maybe the photo dog has a rounder head shape or a coat type that holds a certain look better. Your groomer will explain what will translate and what won't.
3. **"Not today, but here's the path to it."** If the coat has tangles or mats, the groomer may need to go shorter now and grow into the look over a few appointments.

That third answer isn't a failure. Most of the iconic fluffy doodle photos online are dogs on a tight grooming schedule with daily brushing at home. Getting there is a plan, not a single appointment.

A few things to tell the groomer along with the photo:

- How often you realistically brush at home
- Whether you'd rather keep the face longer, shorter, or rounded
- Any spots your dog is sensitive about (feet, face, tail)
- Whether your dog swims, goes to daycare, or spends a lot of time outside

## Choosing a length that fits your routine

The honest rule of thumb: the longer the coat, the more brushing it needs and the more often you'll be seeing your groomer. These are typical ranges many groomers suggest; your groomer will adjust based on your dog's coat.

**Short (summer cut)**
A short all-over cut with a tidy face and tail. Lowest maintenance. Light brushing at home and appointments roughly every 6–8 weeks. Great for active dogs, swimmers, and busy households. Your dog will still look like a doodle, just a sleeker one.

**Medium (teddy bear)**
The style most people picture. Soft and rounded, long enough to look plush. Plan on brushing several times a week with a slicker brush and a metal comb, all the way to the skin, plus appointments every 4–6 weeks. Behind the ears, under the collar, the armpits, and the legs tangle first.

**Long and fluffy**
Beautiful and a real commitment. Daily brushing to the skin, appointments around every 4 weeks, and a willingness to go shorter if life gets busy. Long coats also pick up more burrs, mud, and water, so this works best for dogs that spend most of their time indoors.

If you're between two lengths, pick the shorter one and grow it out if you find you're keeping up. It's a lot easier to add length over a few visits than to deal with a matted long coat.

## How coat condition changes the appointment

Doodle coats mat from the skin outward, which means a dog can look fluffy on top and be tightly matted underneath. When you run a comb through the coat and it stops, that's a mat.

If your doodle arrives with matting, here's what to expect:

- **The groomer assesses the whole coat first.** Where the mats are, how tight they are, and how close to the skin.
- **Options get narrower.** Light tangles can often be worked through. Tight mats, especially large areas of them, usually need to be clipped under rather than brushed out, because brushing through a tight mat is painful and hard on the skin. In that case, a shorter cut is the kind choice.
- **Time goes up.** PRR's haircuts are priced on hands-on grooming time with a one-hour minimum, so a coat that takes longer to work through costs more than one that's brush-ready.
- **The groomer will talk through options with you.** The goal is a comfortable dog and a realistic plan for the next visit, not a lecture.

If you know the coat has gotten away from you, say so when you book. It helps the groomer plan the appointment, and nobody at PRR is going to judge you for it. Doodle coats get ahead of a lot of good owners.

## Planning ongoing grooming

Doodles do best on a recurring schedule rather than "when the coat looks like it needs it." PRR's mobile clients keep a recurring 4-, 6-, or 8-week schedule, and mobile appointments typically book 1–3 weeks out depending on your area, so it pays to set the next appointment before the current one ends.

Between visits, the single most useful habit is a slicker-and-comb session a few times a week, focused on the trouble spots. Skip bathing a tangled dog at home; water tightens tangles into mats. And if you're ever unsure whether something is a tangle or a mat, bring it up at your next appointment.
