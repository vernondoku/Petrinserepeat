Nail trims are the chore most dog owners put off. The dog hates it, you're afraid of cutting too short, and before long you can hear clicking on the kitchen floor. If that sounds familiar, you're in good company, and it's exactly why a quick trip to a groomer for nails alone is one of the most common things people book.

This post walks through what Pet Rinse Repeat offers for nail care at the North Kansas City salon and in the mobile vans, what to tell us when you book, how our groomers handle dogs who don't like their paws touched, and when it makes sense to fold nails into a full groom instead.

## How to book and what to tell us

Booking is direct: you pick a real time slot online, not a request that someone confirms later. When you book, use the appointment notes to tell us a few things:

- **How your dog handles paw touching.** Totally fine, wiggly, pulls away, or has snapped before. We need the honest version.
- **Any past nail-trim trouble.** A nail cut too short once can make a dog wary for a long time. If that happened, say so.
- **Whether your dog has ever bitten or growled at a groomer or vet.** PRR charges a $300 fee when a biting or reactive history isn't disclosed up front. It's a safety issue, and telling us ahead of time just means we plan the appointment differently.
- **Dark nails or dewclaws.** Not a problem, just useful to know.
- **Anything medical** your groomer should work around: arthritis, a sore leg, recent surgery.

## What happens during the visit

A nail trim is a short appointment. The groomer will meet your dog, let them settle for a moment, and then work through each paw. Depending on the dog and the nail, that may mean clippers, a dremel (a small rotary file that grinds the nail down and smooths the edge), or both. Many groomers prefer a dremel for dogs who dislike the pressure of clippers, and it tends to leave a smoother finish with less chance of a sharp edge.

If you've booked the Paw-ticure, the groomer will also trim the hair that grows between and around the pads. On a lot of dogs, especially fluffy-footed breeds, that hair mats, picks up ice and salt in a Kansas City winter, and makes them slide on hard floors. Clearing it out is a small thing that makes a noticeable difference in traction.

One thing worth knowing: a groomer can only take nails back so far in one visit. The quick (the living part inside the nail) grows out with the nail, so a dog whose nails have been long for months will need a few trims spaced a couple of weeks apart to get them back to a comfortable length. That isn't the groomer being cautious for the sake of it; it's how nails work.

## Dogs who hate having their paws touched

This is most dogs, to some degree, so don't feel bad about it.

PRR groomers work one-on-one and will slow down for a nervous dog: approaching the paw gradually, taking breaks between feet, switching from clippers to a dremel or back if one is clearly tolerated better, and keeping the whole thing as brief as possible. For some dogs, the most useful first visit is one where the groomer gets two paws done calmly and leaves the other two for next time. Building trust tends to pay off more than forcing all four.

There is a limit, though. A dog who is biting or thrashing hard enough to risk injury won't be forced through it. In that case the groomer will stop, finish whatever can be done safely, and talk with you about what to try next. That might be a different approach, a different setting, some handling practice at home, or a conversation with your vet. We don't give sedation advice, and nothing here is a substitute for your veterinarian's guidance.

At home, a little practice between appointments helps more than people expect. Hold a paw for a second, treat, let go. Touch a nail with the back of a spoon, treat. Keep it short and boring. If your dog is anxious about grooming generally, our post on [grooming an anxious dog](/blog/grooming-an-anxious-dog/) goes deeper.

## Nails alone, or part of a full groom?

A standalone nail appointment is the right call when:

- Your dog's coat is in good shape and only the nails need attention.
- You want a quick, low-pressure introduction to the salon before committing to a full groom.
- You're between regular grooms and the nails have gotten ahead of the schedule.
- Your dog is nervous and you want to build up slowly.

Including nail care in a full-service groom makes more sense when the coat needs work anyway. A full groom at PRR includes a breed-appropriate haircut, bath and blow dry, de-shedding as needed, ear cleaning, and a sanitary trim, with nail trimming as an add-on. If you're already booking a groom, adding nails means one trip instead of two.

How often nails need doing varies a lot by dog. Most groomers suggest somewhere in the range of every three to six weeks, with dogs who walk mostly on grass or carpet needing more frequent trims than dogs who spend time on pavement. Your groomer can give you a better read after seeing your dog's nails.
